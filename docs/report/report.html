1 · SummaryWhat was built, and what it shows

The project answers questions about aerial images (“Is there a road on the right of a park?”, “How many buildings are there?”) and explains each answer. It was built in four steps, each tested against a rule fixed before its results came in.

PartWhat it doesMain result
Hybrid modelA small CNN turns the 512×512 tile into patches, a Transformer relates them, and the question’s words attend over them. Two heads: one picks an answer, one predicts a count.90.2% accuracy and a count error of 0.91 on the test set; 76.9% and 4.90 on Philadelphia.
CLIP baselineA large general image-text model used with no training on this data.Below even “always give the most common answer”. Our model leads by 40.8 points (test) and 21.9 (Philadelphia).
Explanation maps (XAI)Five ways to show which parts of the image an answer depended on, each tested by blanking those parts and rerunning the model.Only occlusion passed every test, on both test sets. Attention maps and Grad-CAM failed.
RAG explanationsFor each answer, find the 5 most similar training questions, show their true answers, and warn when they disagree. An LLM writes that part; code checks it.The neighbours flag wrong answers better than random ones on every question type. A warned answer is 3–4× as likely to be wrong.
The three findings worth remembering
  • It works well where it was trained, and much less well in a new city. Accuracy falls 13 points in Philadelphia, mostly on area questions (86.1% → 64.8%); counts there barely beat always guessing the median.
  • Most explanation maps don’t survive testing. Attention looks convincing but changes the answer no more than random cells; only occlusion is faithful. Even occlusion shows what the model depended on, not where an object is.
  • Retrieval gives a useful, honest warning, but a partial one. When it fires, take it seriously. It fires on 1–12% of questions, so most wrong answers are not warned.
What this report does not claim That the architecture beats a CLIP model fine-tuned on this data (only zero-shot CLIP was tested); that the maps point at the right object (the dataset has no object outlines); or that the RAG explanations help people (a blind rating was designed but not carried out).

How the work was done

  • Rules before results. Every comparison (which checkpoint, which map, whether retrieval helps, when to warn) was fixed before its numbers were seen, so results could not be chosen to fit.
  • No leakage. Splits are by image tile, and every statistic (vocabularies, image mean and spread) is fitted on the training split only.
  • Built for size, cheap to run. Data is streamed and each tile decoded once; 206 automated tests, all passing. Training ran on one Colab T4 GPU; the LLM is a free hosted API, called 80 times in total.

3 · The dataRSVQA: questions about aerial tiles

RSVQA pairs aerial image tiles with automatically generated questions and answers, built from OpenStreetMap. Two versions were used.

The four HR question types

TypeExampleAnswerHandled as
PresenceIs a small commercial building present?yes / noclassification
ComparisonAre there more buildings than water areas?yes / noclassification
AreaWhat is the area covered by residential buildings?exact m², e.g. 1234m2classification into 5 ranges: 0, 1–10, 11–100, 101–1000, over 1000 m²
CountHow many medium buildings are there?a numberregression: a separate head predicts the number

Decisions about the data

  • Two test sets. test comes from the same region as training. test_phili is Philadelphia, a city the model never sees: it measures whether the model generalises.
  • Area as ranges, count as a number. Area answers are exact square metres, so they were grouped into 5 ranges, giving 7 possible answers in total (yes, no and the 5 ranges). Counts stay numbers: treating 7 and 8 as unrelated classes would throw away their order.
  • Splits by tile. All questions about one tile stay in one split. Otherwise the model could be tested on a tile it trained on.
  • Training-only statistics. The word vocabulary, the answer list and the image normalisation are fitted on training tiles only.
  • Streaming. The full dataset does not fit in memory; tiles are read when needed and decoded once into a cache file (about 0.8 MB per tile on disk), so each of a tile’s ~100 questions does not decode it again.
A data quirk found later: no-data stripsSome tiles, mostly in Philadelphia, have black strips where no imagery exists (33% of sampled Philadelphia questions, against 2% on test). They matter for the explanation maps (section 9) but do not explain the Philadelphia drop: accuracy on strip tiles is not consistently lower.

4 · The modelA hybrid CNN-Transformer with two heads

A CNN is good at local texture (roofs, roads, trees); a Transformer is good at relations across the image (“on the right of”). The model uses both, then lets each word of the question look at the image.

Why these choices

  • Cross-attention fusion. The first version squeezed the image into one summary vector and joined it with the question. Letting each question word attend over the 256 image patches works better (section 5) and gives an attention map to test later. The original version is kept as an option for comparison.
  • Two heads, one backbone. Counting is a different job from choosing an answer, so it gets its own output. Both heads read the same fused vector, which keeps the model small.
  • Count trained on log(1 + count). Raw counts made the count loss millions of times larger than the answer loss, drowning it (section 5).
  • Small on purpose. A model with 3.7× the parameters was tried in Week 3 and could not be trained stably (section 6).
  • Everything in one config file. Image size, model sizes, answer list and training settings live in configs/hr_config.yaml; moving from LR to HR was a config change, not a rewrite.

Problem found in Week 1: the count head was not learningAccuracy rose every epoch (55.5% → 75.7%) while count error stayed flat. Raw LR counts reach the thousands, so the count loss was hundreds of thousands of times larger than the answer loss and dominated training. It was a scaling problem, not a lack of capacity.

6 · Weeks 2–3Moving to HR, and tuning

HR has 14 times the tiles at 4 times the pixels. Pilot runs on 1,500 training tiles were used to fix speed and stability before any tuning.

Making it fast enough

The first pilot took about an hour per epoch: every question decoded its 512×512 image again on Colab’s 2 CPU cores. Decoding each tile once into a cache, and normalising images on the GPU, took loading from 67 to 726 images per second; mixed precision then brought pilot epochs from ~38 to ~22 minutes.

Making it stable before tuning

Two identical pilot runs differed by 4–6 points, and validation swung between epochs (88% → 79% → 88% → 69%). A difference that size would swamp any tuning result. The fixes: a fixed seed, a warm-up and cosine learning-rate schedule, a checkpoint score that counts both accuracy and count error, and no early stopping. After that, three seeds of the same setting landed within 0.1 points of each other (89.1–89.2% validation).

Why the wide model failed. It led the small model for its first 2–3 epochs (85.6% vs 80.8% at epoch 2), then validation collapsed to 39–51%. A diagnostic script ruled out the two usual suspects: the CNN’s normalisation statistics and float16 overflow. Something inside the larger Transformer diverges.

Decision: keep the small model. A known fix (a “pre-norm” Transformer) is an architecture change needing its own tuning round, so it is left as future work.

Learning rate: 3e-4 has ~0.5 points more accuracy, 1.5e-4 a lower count error. 1.5e-4 was kept because the selection score was fixed before tuning.

8 · Week 5Comparison with zero-shot CLIP

CLIP (clip-vit-large-patch14) matches images with text. With no training on RSVQA, it scores one sentence per possible answer (“Is a road present? yes”) against the tile and takes the best. The sentence template was chosen on validation; each test set was scored once.

  • Zero-shot CLIP is below the trivial guess on every type, in both test sets. Our model leads it by 40.8 points on test and 21.9 on Philadelphia.
  • This is a low bar. It shows a model trained for the task beats an untrained general one. It does not show our architecture beats a CLIP model fine-tuned on RSVQA, which was not tested.
  • Not like for like. CLIP sees the tile at 224×224 (about 34 cm per pixel against our 15 cm), which hurts it; it only chooses among answers valid for the question type, which helps it.

9 · Week 5Explaining the answers: which maps can be trusted?

A heatmap over the image is the usual way to “explain” a vision model. But a map can look plausible and still not reflect what the model used. So every map was tested before being shown.

The test

Deletion test, rule fixed before any resultBlank the cells a map ranks highest and rerun the model. If the map is faithful, the answer should change more than when blanking the same number of random cells. A map qualifies only if it beats random on every question type and passes two sanity checks: it must change when the model’s weights are randomised (correlation below 0.5), and it must differ between two questions about the same tile (correlation below 0.9). Among qualifying maps, the one with the largest effect is chosen. Sample: up to 250 right and 250 wrong answers per type and test set (3,829 questions).

Five maps, one survivor

MapWhat it isResult
Cross-attentionwhere the question’s words attendfails no better than random on any type
Attention rolloutattention traced back through the encoderfails beats random, but is nearly the same map for different questions (0.967); ties random in Philadelphia
Grad-CAMgradients at the last CNN stagefails beats random on count only; blank on 314 of 3,829
Gradient-weighted attentionattention weighted by its gradient (Chefer et al., 2021)qualifies but a much smaller effect
Occlusiongrey out each of the 256 cells in turn, measure how much the answer changeschosen chosen on validation, then confirmed on both test sets
  • It holds in Philadelphia, the unseen city, where rollout tied random. Count is the weakest type there.
  • The test favours occlusion somewhat, since both measure sensitivity to removing cells. Blanking was switched from grey to a blur to reduce this, and the other maps were rerun the same way: they still fail.
  • It is slow: 256 model runs per answer, 1.48 s on a T4, against 0.009 s for attention maps.

9 · Week 5, continuedWhat the maps look like

Brighter cells mattered more. The same tile and question under all four first maps; tiles were picked by a fixed rule, not for looks.

How to read these maps“Changing these cells changed the answer most”, not “the model looked at this object”. The strongest cells are mostly scattered and do not trace the object asked about. Occlusion changes one cell at a time, so an object spread over many cells (a road, a block of houses) may show weakly. The dataset has no object outlines, so whether a map points at the right object cannot be measured.

10 · Week 6RAG explanations

Retrieval-augmented explanation: for each answer, find the training questions the model treats as most similar, show what their true answers were, and warn when they disagree with the model. Nothing new is trained.

Step 1: does retrieval find anything useful?

Before writing any explanation, retrieval itself was tested. For one question per test tile and type (13,128 questions): does disagreement between the model and its 5 neighbours flag the model’s wrong answers better than disagreement with 5 random training questions of the same type? Rule fixed in advance: the difference must have a 95% bootstrap lower bound above zero.

  • Count’s warning is mostly about size: random neighbours already reach 0.778, because a large count disagrees with almost anything and large counts are more often wrong. Retrieval adds less there.
  • The neighbours mostly agree with the model (79–95% of the time), as expected, since the heads read the same vector. So retrieval is a second opinion from the same model’s memory, not a better model.
  • Similarity scores are not shown: nearest neighbours score 0.99–1.00 on every type, so the scale says little. Explanations give a neighbour’s rank (#1 to #5) instead.

10 · Week 6, continuedThe uncertainty warning

Rule, fixed before any result: a yes/no or area answer is warned when fewer than 3 of its 5 neighbours have it as their true answer; a count is warned when, rounded, it lies outside the neighbours’ range of true counts.

When it warns, take it seriouslyA warned answer is about 3–4× as likely to be wrong (count about 2.4×). This holds in both test sets.
Silence is not a guaranteeIt fires on only 1–12% of questions, so most wrong answers are not warned. Count almost never warns, because the neighbours’ range is usually wide.

10 · Week 6, continuedThe explanation text

Step 3: the explanation

Everything that is a plain fact is written by code. The LLM (openai/gpt-oss-120b on Groq’s free tier) writes one paragraph about the neighbours, from a facts sheet that never includes the true answer or whether the model was right. Code checks the paragraph and replaces it with a fixed template if it uses a number not in the facts, cites a neighbour that does not exist, says what is in the image, gives reasons for the answer (“based on”, “the model sees”), calls anything correct or wrong, runs over 90 words, or adds or leaves out the warning against the rule.

Example (a warned count question; the true answer was 71). Grey: written by code. Yellow: written by the LLM, checked.

The model answered “4”. The model’s answer is uncertain because its count of 4 lies outside the range of true answers (6 to 12) seen in similar cases. Among the five most similar training questions, #1 asks “What is the amount of buildings?” with a true answer of 6, and #5 asks the same question with a true answer of 9; none of the similar cases have a true answer of 4. Changing the cells at the top centre and top left of the tile changed the answer most. That tells you what the answer depended on, not where anything is.
Full runQuestionsLLM paragraph passed the checksFell back to the template
Main set (balanced by type, test set and right/wrong)64604
Warned set16115

80 requests, 11.5 minutes, within the free tier. Three pilot rounds shaped the design: the first prompt made the LLM invent training cases it was not given, blame the warning on the model’s confidence, and claim the neighbours caused the answer. Each problem got a rule and a check.

Limits of the RAG layer
  • Unanimous neighbours can back a wrong answer. When all 5 share the model’s wrong answer, the paragraph makes it sound well supported.
  • Some LLM statements are not checked, such as arithmetic on the facts (“none of the similar cases has 4”).
  • Whether the explanations help people was not measured. A blind rating (80 pairs, identical except for the neighbour paragraph, decision rule fixed in advance) was designed and generated but not carried out.

11 · Limitations, and what comes nextWhere the results stop

LimitationConsequence
Large drop in an unseen city (−13 points; area −21)The model should not be trusted on new regions without retraining or adaptation.
Count in Philadelphia barely beats the median guess (4.90 vs 5.25)Counts there are close to uninformative.
Only zero-shot CLIP was comparedNo claim that the architecture beats a fine-tuned foundation model.
The larger model could not be trained stablyWhether more capacity helps is unknown; a pre-norm Transformer is the next thing to try.
One seed for the full runSmall differences (under ~1 point) between full-run results are not established.
No object outlines in RSVQAExplanation maps are tested for faithfulness, not for pointing at the right object.
Occlusion behaves oddly at no-data stripsExplanations add a fixed caution on strip tiles.
RAG warning misses most errors; human rating not runPresent the warning as a “be careful” signal; make no claim about helping users.
RSVQA answers come from OpenStreetMapLabels are generated automatically and can be wrong or out of date; accuracy is measured against them.

Week 7: the demo app

  • A local web app that runs on a MacBook (M1, 8 GB): pick a tile, ask a question, see the answer, the occlusion map and the explanation.
  • A fixed set of demo tiles with pre-computed explanations, so the demo needs no GPU and no network; live LLM calls only for new questions, with the template as fallback.
  • Optional: rewriting free-form questions into the RSVQA question style, always showing the rewrite on screen.
  • Error analysis and ablations (for example, concat against cross-attention fusion), a backup demo video, and a short animation for the README.
